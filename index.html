A multi-scale information geometry reveals the structure of mutual information in neural populations

A representational geometry whose distances are mutual information. It stretches the stimulus changes a population encodes and squeezes the ones it ignores, from fine detail to global structure, and it can be estimated for natural images with a diffusion model.

The multi-scale geometry changes structure that natural images contain (brightness, edges, the eye); the Fisher information changes pixel noise that natural images rarely contain.

Exact link to information

Averaged over stimuli, half the trace of the metric is the mutual information: $I(R;X) = \tfrac12\langle \operatorname{tr} G(x)\rangle$. Fisher information gives it only for large populations.

Unique from first principles

It is the only geometry whose distances contract under stimulus blurring at a rate set by the local response statistics.

Practical for natural images

A conditional diffusion model returns the metric for high-dimensional stimuli. In V1 and V4 its leading directions are stable across encoding models; Fisher's are not.

The idea in one neuron

The Fisher information measures how well a code separates nearby stimuli. Mutual information also depends on how well it separates stimuli that are far apart. One neuron with three tuning curves shows the difference.

The monotonic and single-peaked neurons have the same slope everywhere, so their Fisher information and their discrimination of nearby stimuli are identical. But the single-peaked neuron answers the same way on both sides of its peak, so it carries less information. Blurring the stimulus by $t$ and adding the Fisher information that survives at every scale gives the multi-scale metric $$G(x)=\int_0^\infty \big\langle J_t(x_t)\big\rangle_{p(x_t\mid x)}\,dt ,$$ whose distances rank the three neurons as their mutual information does. In this example the identity $I(R;X)=\tfrac12\langle G(x)\rangle_{p(x)}$ can be checked numerically: the bars show both sides.

Estimating it for images

For natural images the blurred likelihood $p_t(r\mid x_t)$ averages over every image consistent with a blurred one, which cannot be computed directly. A conditional diffusion model, trained to denoise images given neural responses, returns the denoised image $\hat x(x_t, r)$; how much it changes between two independent responses estimates $J_t$ at each scale. Averaging over scales, blurred images and responses gives the leading eigenvectors of $G(x)$ (Algorithm 1 of the paper).

Applied to macaque V1 and V4

Multi-unit activity from V1 and V4 of macaques viewing natural images (THINGS, Papale et al. 2025), 50 channels per area, modelled by frozen InceptionV3 or ResNet-50 features with a Gaussian readout. One diffusion model per area and encoder, trained on TinyImageNet and conditioned on the predicted responses with noise matched to trial-to-trial variability.

Results

Different features in V1 and V4

The leading eigenvectors of $G(x)$ are changes that natural images contain: first a change of brightness over the receptive fields, then edges and fine detail in V1, and coarser changes that follow the object's shape in V4. Those of the Fisher information are pixel noise in both areas.

Constrained by the data, not by the model

Two encoding models fitted to the same area agree on $G(x)$ more than one model fitted to the two areas. For the Fisher information the ordering reverses: it reflects the model more than the recorded area. (Subspace alignment of the top $k$ eigenvectors, averaged over 200 held-out images.)

The same at every point of the field

Around a reference image, perturbed along the two leading eigenvectors of $G(x)$, the multi-scale metric of the two models (Inception solid, ResNet-50 dashed) stays close everywhere; the Fisher metric does not.

Use it on your own model

The multigeom package computes both metrics for any fitted PyTorch model of neural responses, and reproduces the paper's analyses.

pip install multigeom
import multigeom

encoder = multigeom.load_encoder("inception", area="V1")   # fitted to macaque V1
fisher = multigeom.compute_fisher(image=x, encoder=encoder,
                                  sigma=0.22, top_k=10)     # J(x)

Abstract

Understanding how neural population responses represent sensory information is a central problem in systems neuroscience. One approach is to define a representational geometry on stimulus space in which distances reflect how reliably stimuli can be distinguished from neural activity. However, different constructions of these distances can lead to qualitatively different conclusions about the neural code. Here, we show that a unique Riemannian representational geometry emerges from first principles governing how distances contract as stimulus resolution is lost through coarse-graining. This results in a multi-scale extension of the Fisher information metric, capturing encoding structure from fine stimulus details to coarse global distinctions. The resulting geometry is exactly related to the mutual information encoded by the population: well encoded stimulus directions, those contributing more to mutual information, are expanded, whereas poorly encoded directions are contracted. The metric tensor can be estimated using diffusion models, making the framework practical for large neural populations and high-dimensional stimuli. Applied to visual cortical responses to natural images, the eigenvectors of the metric tensor identify stimulus variations that contribute most to information transmission, yielding interpretable features that are robust to modelling choices. Together, these results provide a principled, information-theoretic framework for characterising neural population codes.
