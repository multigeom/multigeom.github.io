A multi-scale information geometry reveals the structure of mutual information in neural populations

A representational geometry with an exact link to mutual information: averaged over stimuli, half the trace of its metric equals the information a population carries. It stretches stimulus directions that contribute more to that information and contracts poorly encoded ones, from fine detail to global structure. For natural images it can be estimated with a conditional diffusion model.

Exact link to information

Averaged over stimuli, half the trace of the metric equals the mutual information: $I(R;X) = \tfrac12\langle \operatorname{tr} G(x)\rangle$.

Derived from first principles

The metric follows from how the geometry changes for an observer who sees only a blurred version of the stimulus. Coarse-grain the stimulus by adding Gaussian noise. Up to a constant factor, this is the only metric that contracts at a rate set by the local response distribution and by what the responses encode. It is also zero when the responses carry no information about the stimulus.

Practical for natural images

A conditional diffusion model gives Monte Carlo estimates of the metric's leading eigenvectors for 64×64-pixel images. In V1 and V4 they are robust to differences in the encoding model fitted to the same area; those of the Fisher information are not.

The idea in one neuron

The Fisher information measures how well a code separates nearby stimuli. Mutual information also depends on how well it separates stimuli that are far apart. Three model neurons show the difference. Each encodes a scalar stimulus with a Gaussian distribution, through one tuning curve with the same Gaussian response noise.

The monotonic and single-peaked neurons have the same slope magnitude $|f'(x)|$ at every stimulus, so their Fisher information $J(x)=f'(x)^2/\sigma^2$ and their Fisher distances are identical. Yet the single-peaked neuron responds in the same way to stimuli on either side of its peak, so it carries less mutual information. The low-gain neuron has a quarter of the monotonic neuron's Fisher information everywhere, the least of the three, but it carries more mutual information than the single-peaked one.

The multi-scale metric captures both orderings. It coarse-grains the stimulus by adding Gaussian noise of variance $t$, $x_t = x+\sqrt{t}\,z$. At each scale it takes the Fisher information $J_t$ of the coarse-grained likelihood $p_t(r\mid x_t)$ and averages it over the noisy stimuli. It then integrates over all scales: $$G(x)=\int_0^\infty \big\langle J_t(x_t)\big\rangle_{x_t\sim\mathcal N(x,\,t)}\,dt .$$ For $t>0$, $J_t$ depends on the responses to neighbouring stimuli within about $\sqrt t$ and on the stimulus distribution, so $G$ reflects global as well as local structure. Distances under $G$ rank the three neurons as their mutual information does. The identity $I(R;X)=\tfrac12\langle G(x)\rangle_{p(x)}$ also holds numerically: the bars show the mutual information and the black ticks $\tfrac12\langle G\rangle$.

Estimating it for images

For natural images the coarse-grained likelihood $p_t(r\mid x_t)$ averages over every image that could have produced the noisy image $x_t$, which cannot be computed directly. A conditional diffusion model, trained to denoise images given neural responses, gives the posterior mean $\hat x(x_t, r)=\mathbb E[x\mid x_t, r]$. Using Tweedie's identity, $J_t(x_t)=\tfrac{1}{2t^2}\,\mathbb E\big[(\hat x(x_t,r)-\hat x(x_t,r'))(\hat x(x_t,r)-\hat x(x_t,r'))^\top\big]$ for two independent responses $r, r'$. Responses are drawn at the denoised image $\hat x(x_t)$, an approximation that is most accurate at fine scales. Averaging over scales, noisy images and response pairs gives a low-rank factor of $G(x)$ whose singular vectors are its leading eigenvectors (Algorithm 1 of the paper).

Applied to macaque V1 and V4

Multi-unit activity recorded with Utah arrays in V1 and V4 of one macaque (one of the two in the dataset) during viewing of 22,248 natural images from THINGS (Papale et al. 2025); the 50 most reliable channels per area. Each area is modelled by frozen ImageNet-trained features (InceptionV3 or ResNet-50) with a per-channel spatial readout and Gaussian response noise close to the trial-to-trial variability ($\sigma=0.22$). Test correlations are 0.75 (V1) and 0.71 (V4) for Inception, 0.78 and 0.70 for ResNet-50. One conditional diffusion model is trained per area and encoder, on 100,000 grayscale 64×64 TinyImageNet images, each paired with the encoder's predicted responses plus noise of the same $\sigma$.

Results

Different features in V1 and V4

The leading eigenvectors of $G(x)$ are image changes of the kind natural images contain. The first is a local change of brightness over the receptive fields. The next ones are edges and fine detail in V1, and broader changes that follow the object's shape in V4. The leading eigenvectors of the Fisher information resemble high-frequency noise confined to the receptive fields, with no clear difference between the two areas.

Constrained by the data, not by the model

Two encoding models fitted to the same area agree on the leading eigenvectors of $G(x)$ more than one model fitted to the two areas. For the Fisher information the ordering reverses, so its leading eigenvectors depend more on the encoding model than on the recorded area. (Subspace alignment of the top $k$ eigenvectors, $k=2$ to 50, averaged over 200 held-out images.)

The same at every point of the field

The reference image is perturbed along the two leading eigenvectors of $G(x)$, and at each perturbed image both metrics are projected onto that plane. The multi-scale ellipses change smoothly and stay broadly consistent between the two models (Inception solid, ResNet-50 dashed). The Fisher ellipses change rapidly and differ between models.

Use it on your own model

The multigeom package computes both metrics for any fitted PyTorch model of neural responses, and reproduces the paper's analyses.

pip install multigeom
import multigeom

encoder = multigeom.load_encoder("inception", area="V1")   # an encoder of the paper, fitted to macaque V1
responses = encoder.predict(images)                          # [N, 50] predicted neural responses, images [N, 1, 64, 64]

# The Fisher information J(x) of the encoder at an image x:
fisher = multigeom.compute_fisher(image=x, encoder=encoder, sigma=0.22, top_k=10)

Abstract

Understanding how neural population responses represent sensory information is a central problem in systems neuroscience. One approach is to define a representational geometry on stimulus space in which distances reflect how reliably stimuli can be distinguished from neural activity. However, different constructions of these distances can lead to qualitatively different conclusions about the neural code. Here, we show that a unique Riemannian representational geometry emerges from first principles governing how distances contract as stimulus resolution is lost through coarse-graining. This results in a multi-scale extension of the Fisher information metric, capturing encoding structure from fine stimulus details to coarse global distinctions. The resulting geometry is exactly related to the mutual information encoded by the population: well encoded stimulus directions, those contributing more to mutual information, are expanded, whereas poorly encoded directions are contracted. The metric tensor can be estimated using diffusion models, making the framework practical for large neural populations and high-dimensional stimuli. Applied to visual cortical responses to natural images, the eigenvectors of the metric tensor identify stimulus variations that contribute most to information transmission, yielding interpretable features that are robust to modelling choices. Together, these results provide a principled, information-theoretic framework for characterising neural population codes.
